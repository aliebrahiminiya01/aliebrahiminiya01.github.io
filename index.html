01

Brain tumor segmentation in MRI

PyTorch · MONAI · 2D U-Net · 484 patients

A U-Net that labels every pixel of a brain MRI as edema, non-enhancing tumor or enhancing tumor, trained on the Medical Segmentation Decathlon brain tumor set. I split the data by patient rather than by slice — otherwise neighbouring slices of the same person leak into the test set and the scores look better than they are. Training ran on my MacBook Air, about four and a half minutes per epoch.

The part I learned most from was the failure analysis. The three worst test slices all came from one patient, where the tumor is barely visible in any of the four MRI sequences and the scan has a strong intensity bias. A 3D model and bias-field correction are the next things I'd try.

02

Predicting meal no-shows at a university restaurant

scikit-learn · pandas · 52,333 reservations

About one in seven reserved meals is never picked up, and every one of them is wasted food. My first model scored 85% accuracy, which sounded fine until I noticed that always predicting “picked up” scores the same. It had caught 51 of 1,563 no-shows.

What actually helped was each student's own history, computed only from the days before each reservation so the model never sees the future, plus testing on the last weeks of the semester instead of a random split. ROC AUC went from 0.55 to 0.66. Holidays turned out to be the biggest single risk: more than half of those meals go uncollected.

03

Hotel booking analysis

pandas · Matplotlib · 119,390 bookings

Two years of bookings from a city hotel and a resort in Portugal. Most of the work was making the numbers honest: about 32,000 duplicate rows, a €5,400 room rate that was a typo, and a revenue figure that only means something once you multiply the nightly rate by the number of nights and drop cancelled stays.

The clearest finding: bookings made within a week are cancelled 8% of the time, bookings made months ahead around 40%.

04

Loan approval with a second opinion

scikit-learn · Tkinter · logistic regression + random forest

A desktop app that estimates whether a loan application should be approved. Two models vote; if they disagree, the application goes to a person instead of being decided automatically. The models approve almost every good applicant but also about 40% of the risky ones — which is exactly why I didn't want either of them deciding alone.
